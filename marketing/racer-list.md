# Get Tucked — Racer Seeding List & Personalisation Kit

Companion to [`outreach-drafts.md`](outreach-drafts.md) §1 (racer outreach). The draft's
first line is a fill-in slot: *"[One specific, true sentence — a setup choice they made, a
scratch, a result.]"* This file is how that slot gets filled well — and honestly.

**Target:** ~12 riders who actually post about setup/gear. Quality over count. One badly
personalised send is worse than not sending.

---

## The one rule

**Every hook must be specific, true, and verifiable — or the line doesn't go in.**

The whole app is built on "we don't state what we can't defend." The outreach has to match,
or the first email contradicts the product. A fabricated or vaguely-remembered detail about a
real person is an instant credibility hole, and this community is small and talks. If you
can't verify a hook in five minutes, either research harder or drop that rider — don't
approximate.

Corollary: **the "no obligation" line in the draft is load-bearing.** Personalisation earns
the read; the no-strings framing earns the trust. Keep both.

---

## What makes a good hook (and what kills one)

A good hook proves you actually followed *them*, not that you googled the race an hour ago.

**Reference one of these — the more specific, the better:**

- A **setup choice** they made and talked about ("running a half-frame bag to keep bottle
  access", "the cockpit bag you 3D-printed a mount for").
- A **scratch or mechanical** and how they handled it (respectfully — it's a sore point).
- A **result or split** ("top-10 at [race]", "your Tuesday-to-Friday on the [route]").
- A **route or strategy** decision ("skipping the resupply at X and carrying four days").
- A **gear opinion they've voiced publicly** ("your take on aero bars for loaded riding").

**Never:**

- Generic praise — "love your content", "big fan", "your feed is awesome". Reads like a mail
  merge because it is one.
- Anything you can't verify. If it's a maybe, it's a no.
- Anything about their **body** or how "aero" they personally look — the app measures rigs and
  positions, not people's worth; keep it about the setup.
- Backhanded framing — "your position looked rough on the climbs, our app could help". You're
  offering a tool, not a diagnosis.
- Faux-intimacy — don't imply a friendship that isn't there. One rider noticing another's work
  is the register, not "hey buddy".

Notice: each names a **real, specific, checkable** thing, then pivots to the app in the *same*
sentence. No generic runway.

## Research sources (where to verify the hook)

Fill hooks from these, not from memory:

- **dotwatcher.cc** — race coverage, finisher lists, rider interviews for the big ultras.
- **The race's own results / tracker** (Tour Divide, Trackleaders, etc.) — for real splits and
  scratches.
- **Their own channels** — Instagram, YouTube, a personal blog, Strava (public activities),
  bikepacking.com rider features. This is where setup detail actually lives.
- **bikepacking.com** rig features and race recaps — often quote riders on their exact kit.
- **Podcast appearances** — riders talk setup at length; a 5-minute skim of a show-notes page
  often yields the perfect specific.

Log the **source link** next to each hook so a fact can be re-checked before send.

post setups (higher hit-rate than big accounts).

## Send mechanics

- **Personalise or don't send** — a row without a verified hook doesn't get an email.
- **One rider per email**, sent individually. No BCC blast. This is one-to-one or it's nothing.
- **Keep it short** — the draft is already close to the ceiling. Resist adding a second
  paragraph about features; the methodology link does that job.
- **Space the sends** over a few days so replies are answerable in real time (same logic as
  the Reddit "block out the evening").
- **TestFlight link ready** before the first send (outreach fill-in slot).
- **Privacy/consent:** these are public figures contacted individually about a relevant tool —
  fine. Don't scrape or compile private data; use what they've published. Don't add anyone to a
  list or newsletter — there isn't one, and that's a selling point.
